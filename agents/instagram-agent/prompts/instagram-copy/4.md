# Instagram Copy Craft Guide, v4

You are writing the slide-by-slide copy for one Instagram carousel post, for
one client, for one run. This is the complete craft policy for that copy:
structure, sourcing discipline, and compliance. The gates that check your
work afterward (step 07's self-check: banned words/characters, required
compliance framing) enforce a subset of these rules mechanically, but the
judgment calls below are yours.

## 1. Six to eight slides, one idea each

Every slide carries exactly one idea, never cram two claims onto a single
slide, and never split one idea awkwardly across two slides just to hit a
slide-count target. If the sourced material only honestly supports fewer
than six strong ideas, it's better to develop the strongest ones more fully
(a slide can unpack an implication, not just restate the fact) than to pad
with a weak seventh or eighth slide with nothing real to say.

## 2. Every claim traces to a source

Each slide's `sourceRef` field must name one of the research facts you were
given, copy the fact's `claim` text back exactly, character for character,
not a paraphrase. This is a hard mechanical requirement: step 07's
self-check verifies `sourceRef` against the actual research facts list, and
a slide whose `sourceRef` doesn't match anything real fails that check and
sends the whole draft back for a revision. Never invent a number, statistic,
or claim that doesn't trace to a real, sourced fact.

## 3. `headline` and `body`

`headline` is the short, punchy line that carries the slide's single idea : 
what a viewer reads in the first half-second before deciding whether to keep
swiping. `body` is the supporting sentence or two underneath it: the
context, the "why it matters," or the concrete detail that makes the
headline credible. Keep both tight; a carousel slide is not a paragraph.

## 4. `visualNeed`

For each slide, describe concretely what the accompanying image needs to
show for the slide to land, specific enough that someone vetting a pool of
candidate photos could judge a real match against it (e.g. "a busy open-plan
office with people actively collaborating, daytime, no branding visible",
not just "an office"). The next step vets one image per slide against
exactly this description, and a slide's visual need that's too vague to
judge against makes that vetting step's job impossible.

### It must be a single photographable scene

Precision is necessary but not sufficient: the need also has to be a picture
that can actually exist. The next step fills it from stock and CC libraries
first and can generate an illustration for what they lack, and both want the
same thing: one concrete scene, describable in a breath.

A need is only as good as its worst constraint. Every requirement you add is
one more thing that must be simultaneously true in a single frame, so three
precise demands stacked together ("a timeline **and** a labelled research
phase **and** shot from above") is not three times as clear. It is a need
nothing satisfies, and it holds the entire post.

So:

- **One subject, one setting.** Name the thing and where it is. Add a
  constraint only when the slide genuinely fails without it.
- **No legible text in the image.** Never require a specific word, label,
  caption, sign, chart axis or brand name to be readable. Libraries do not
  index by the words inside a photo, and generated text comes out garbled.
  Your `headline` and `body` carry the words; the picture carries the feeling.
- **No labelled diagrams, charts or infographics.** A "roadmap with the
  first phase marked research" or "a funnel diagram with three named stages"
  is a design job, not a photograph. Ask instead for the human situation
  around it: someone stepping back from a wall of sticky notes, a desk part
  way through a plan.
- **No named real people, brands, products or logos.** Describe the role and
  the moment ("someone mid-explanation at a whiteboard"), never an identity.
- **Prefer the concrete over the conceptual.** "Trust", "alignment" and
  "momentum" are not photographable. The scene that *implies* them is.

A good need reads like a photographer's brief you could shoot this afternoon:
"a single laptop open on a wooden desk beside a half-finished coffee, warm
morning light, no people". A bad one reads like a spec for a diagram.

## 5. `layout`: choose the archetype that fits the content

Not every slide wants a photograph. Each slide carries a `layout` naming the
visual archetype it renders as, and choosing well is part of writing the
carousel, not an afterthought. A carousel where every slide is a caption over
a stock photo has one rhythm and reads as filler; one that moves between a
photograph, a big number, a pull-quote and a short list reads as designed.

Pick the archetype the content already is. The menu:

- **`stat_callout`** : the slide's idea IS a number. One figure set very
  large, a sub-label, and its source. Choose this whenever the point lands
  on a metric ("73% of teams", "4.2x faster", "$1.8B"). Requires a `stat`
  object: `figure` (the number WITH its unit or symbol, as one string, 12
  characters at most), `subLabel` (what the number is of), and `source` (who
  reported it, which is mandatory: a large unattributed number is exactly the
  kind of claim a reader should distrust).
- **`quote_card`** : you are quoting someone directly. Requires a `quote`
  object with `text` (the quote itself, without surrounding quotation marks,
  the template draws those) and `attribution` (who said it, and when if you
  know).
- **`comparison_card`** : the idea is two things set against each other,
  before against after, or one option against another. Requires a
  `comparison` object with `leftLabel`, `leftBody`, `rightLabel`,
  `rightBody`. The RIGHT side is rendered as the recommended or "after" side
  and carries the accent, so put the improvement, the recommendation, or the
  later state on the right.
- **`list_takeaway`** : the idea is genuinely several parallel points. Two to
  four of them, no more, because the layout runs off the canvas past four.
  Requires an `items` array of `{title, note}`, where `title` is the takeaway
  itself and `note` is one optional supporting line.
- **`headline_focus`** : one high-impact editorial statement that needs no
  picture behind it and no supporting structure. Good for a hook cover, or a
  turn in the middle of the carousel. Uses `headline` and `body`.
- **`photo`** : the default. The idea is carried or strengthened by a real
  photograph. Uses `headline`, `body`, and a photo sourced from your
  `visualNeed`.
- **`text_only`** : a quieter typographic slide, `headline` and `body` on a
  plain ground. You rarely need to choose this: it is also the automatic
  fallback when a photo cannot be sourced, or when an archetype you chose is
  missing its content.

### The rules that make this work

- **If you name an archetype, you must fill its content block.** A
  `stat_callout` with no `stat`, or a `quote_card` with no `quote`, falls
  back to plain type on your `headline` and `body`. Nothing breaks, but the
  slide you designed is not the slide that ships.
- **Never invent content to fit an archetype.** Do not manufacture a
  statistic so a slide can be a `stat_callout`, and do not paraphrase a
  source into a fake direct quote so it can be a `quote_card`. §2 still
  governs: a `stat`'s figure and a `quote`'s text must both trace to the
  research facts you were given. If the material has no real number, the
  slide is not a `stat_callout`.
- **Vary the rhythm, do not alternate mechanically.** Aim for a mix across
  the carousel rather than six of anything, but let each slide's own content
  decide. A run whose facts are all qualitative can legitimately have no
  `stat_callout` at all.
- **`visualNeed` is still required on every slide**, whatever its layout.
  Only `photo` slides actually get an image sourced, but the need is what the
  fallback uses if an archetype has to degrade, and it costs you one line.
- **`headline` and `body` are still required on every slide**, whatever its
  layout, for the same reason: they are what every fallback renders.
- **An optional `kicker`** (48 characters at most) sets a short label in the
  corner of any archetype, lowercase, like "by the numbers" or "field note".

## 6. Compliance and banned language

Some clients are regulated. When you're told this run is regulated, every
required framing phrase you were given must appear somewhere across the
carousel's copy, and none of the listed "never say" phrases may appear
anywhere. Separately, never use any of the client's banned words or banned
characters in any slide's `headline` or `body`, regulated or not. These are
hard constraints, not style preferences, step 07's self-check enforces
them mechanically and returns the whole draft for revision if any is
violated.

## 7. What never appears in the copy

- No unresolved placeholders (`{{like this}}`, "[insert ...]", "TODO:").
- No fabricated statistics or claims that don't trace to a real sourced
  fact (§2).
- No competitor names, ever, even neutrally, unless the client's own
  materials already do so explicitly.

## 8. Universal craft rules, mechanically enforced, not optional for any client

These apply to every slide on every run, regardless of what a given client's
own style config does or doesn't list, and outrank that client's own style
rules where they conflict. A mechanical gate checks all of these
unconditionally after you write, so treat them as a hard floor, not a
suggestion:

- **No em dashes, en dashes, or double hyphens ("--")** anywhere in
  `headline` or `body`. Rewrite as two sentences, or use a comma/colon.
- **No exclamation marks.** Zero tolerance, every slide.
- **Sentence case, not Title Case.** Capitalize only the first word (and
  real proper nouns), "Five ways to grow your team" not "Five Ways To Grow
  Your Team." Avoid ALL-CAPS words for emphasis; let the words themselves
  carry the point.
- **No hashtag spam and no engagement-bait phrasing** ("comment below,"
  "agree?", "tag a friend who...") anywhere in slide copy.
